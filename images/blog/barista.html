learning to pull espresso, one terrible shot at a time

Barista Training · Life

I started learning to make coffee because I wanted to do something that required my hands and not my brain. That sounds simple. It turned out to be more interesting than that.

Pulling a good espresso shot requires attention in a way that's different from most of the work I do. There's no iterating after the fact. The grind, the tamp, the pressure, the timing — everything matters and you find out immediately whether you got it right. The cup doesn't lie.

I spent November burning through bags of beans and slowly getting less bad at it. I learned how to read the pour. I learned that small adjustments — a fraction of a gram, a second of difference — change everything. I learned that the same variables on two different days produce different results, and that you have to adapt in real time.

There's something meditative about it. You're not multitasking. You're just making coffee. And when you make a good one, there's this quiet satisfaction that's completely out of proportion to the act itself.

But the part I didn't expect was how much it taught me about small interactions. Handing someone a drink made exactly the way they wanted it, first thing in the morning — that's not nothing. You can genuinely make someone's day in thirty seconds. Most of the time people move through those moments without noticing, but being on the making side of it made me start paying more attention. The small things compound. A little care in a small exchange goes further than it seems.

I'm still not great at latte art. But I'm getting there.
