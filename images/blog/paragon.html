Mar 2024 – Jun 2024

40,000 state employees and the question of who gets to write AI policy

Paragon · Generative AI Policy Fellow · Atlanta, GA

When I joined Paragon as a Generative AI Policy Fellow, I had just started my AI and ML classes at school. I was excited about the technology, but I also realized how quickly this problem could roll into something much bigger than a homework problem. It felt like the right time to learn how policy, practice, and product need to work together.

My work was focused on the state government — specifically on drafting policy guidance that would affect over 40,000 state employees. These are people managing public health records, processing benefits, running infrastructure. The stakes of getting AI policy wrong for them are very real, and we weren't always equipped to handle the scale or the uncertainty.

Most of my time was spent researching. I dug into national frameworks — executive orders, federal agency guidance, emerging standards — and mapped how other countries were approaching the same questions. The EU AI Act, the UK's pro-innovation stance, China's generative AI regulations: each offered a different lens on the same core tensions between safety, utility, and civil rights.

The harder challenge was translation. Lawmakers aren't AI researchers. My job wasn't to write a technical paper — it was to turn complex, fast-moving AI concepts into clear, actionable language that a legislator could actually use to make decisions. That meant cutting jargon, grounding everything in concrete scenarios, and being honest about what we don't yet know.

It pushed me to think about AI not just as a product problem, but as a governance problem. And it made me a lot more thoughtful about what "responsible AI" actually requires when you're operating at the scale of a state.
